## PART 2 — THE GRIND

> **[1:30 – 3:30]**
> **[SLIDE: Single bold word: "THE GRIND" — dark background, minimal.]**

My reaction to this was to fight back and prove to them I could do anything. So driven by a need to prove them wrong, I threw myself into obsessive practice. I kept playing football until I was one of the best in my school, I kept cycling not until I was good, but until I didn't know any other kid in the neighbourhood that was better at it. The same was true for swimming, where I competed against abled body kids. I simply couldn't bear the thought that other people would think that I couldn't do something just because of my leg. Showing off, by being very good at things, was my way of convincing others not to exclude me from activities.

I call that period of my life My Grind.

> **[SLIDE: Montage of 4–5 small photos: cycling a mountain pass, swimming, a Himalayan summit, a still from Survivor. No text.]**

Because of the grind, I feel I have reached a point where the term 'disabled' no longer practically applies to me. What are considered hard physical challenges for most are well within my comfort zone. I've walked 100km in a single day. I've cycled the legendary cols of the Tour de France—the Ventoux, the Tourmalet, the Croix de Fer. I've summited 6,000-meter peaks in the Himalayas and survived weeks on a remote island during a television program called Survivor.

> **[SLIDE: Text — "A framework. 4 principles."]**

My grind reshaped what my body can do. But it also reshaped what my mind can take. To get as good as others (while being disadvantaged) I had to push myself very hard and in doing so I developed a framework. A set of 4 principles that I still use today. These 4 principles helped me through a PhD in robotics, they helped me in starting up a business and they are helping now driving me toward gold in LA.

The good news is, you don't need a prosthetic leg to use these principles, these principles work, even for people that have two normal legs. Especially for those with a dream they haven't dared to chase yet.

### PRINCIPLE 1 — You are not amazing, but you might become.

> **[3:30 – 6:00]**
> **[SLIDE: Bold text: "You are not amazing. But you might become." — white on dark background.]**

The first principle is about believing in yourself, I know I sound like a ..  — not because you are amazing, but because amazing things are usually achieved by ordinary people. That's because huge challenges can be broken down into many small steps, each of which, on its own, is easy to take.

Many of you might have a dream. Maybe it's starting a business. Maybe you want to advance your career. Some of you might want to learn how to play guitar so you can give guitar concertos, or run a marathon. These things are definitely challenging. If you have never played guitar and you have to play a guitar concerto tomorrow evening, you are probably going to suck. Here's the shift I want you to make: don't be afraid to take on challenging quests. If your dream is actually possible, see it as the combination of many small steps that anyone, including you, can take.

> **[SLIDE: Photo of the Kulibrie drone in flight.]**

One of my most challenging projects was the development of the Kulibrie. A drone that looks like a hummingbird and flies, not by using propellers as most drones use, but by flapping its tiny little wings. It was the result of my PhD research and it took me 6 years to develop. I had to invent or learn everything from scratch. The flapping mechanism, the tiny electronics, the control software, everything. I had no prior knowledge of how to do these things, but that didn't scare me. I mapped the research path out into tiny doable weekly challenges that all together compounded to this beautiful one of a kind creature.

### PRINCIPLE 2 — Go for it

> **[6:00 – 9:00]**
> **[SLIDE: Large text: "What if I fail?" — centred.]**

I hear this question all the time: "What if I fail?". Fair enough, you want to achieve all challenges you take on. (many reasons… i prefer to try a lot of challenges, if 1 on 5  go well, i still did some cool stuff) Well I know a thing or two about failing. And I think this is the wrong question to ask yourself.

> **[SLIDE: Photo of the Kulibrie product / startup era — packaging, team, or lab photo.]**

From the start of my PhD I planned to develop the Kulibrie as a product, not just as a prototype, because then I might be able to start a company and try to sell them as toys or gadgets. For some reason, starting a company specialised in flapping wing technology sounded like a good idea to me. Step by step, the plan slowly became reality.

I knew it was going to be hard to sell a million robotic hummingbirds. On the one hand it is a complex hardware product. It's almost like mass manufacturing a watch and on the other hand it would be a consumer product, so large investments in marketing were required and distribution channels needed to be set up. I knew chances of success would be small.

Every rational signal (and every expert) was telling me: don't start. The odds are terrible. You'll fail, you'll look naive.

But I tried anyway. I would have regretted it for the rest of my life if I didn't and I also knew: success was a possible outcome. Unlikely — but not impossible. And more importantly, I asked myself the right question. Not "what if this fails?" — but "what is the actual worst case that could happen and can I overcome it?" So I planned the project in such a way that even in the worst case scenario …  And then I started the company, found partners and for two years we gave all we had.

> **[SLIDE: Plain text, centred: "We never sold a single robotic hummingbird."]**

We never sold a single robotic hummingbird.

By most definitions, that's a failure. And yet — standing here today, I can tell you that starting that company was one of the best decisions I've ever made. Because the things I learned during that adventure prepared me for the adventures I am starting today or tomorrow.

And that's exactly the point.

> **[SLIDE: Two lines — "What if I try and it doesn't work out?" and below it: "What if I had tried?"]**

Because there are two versions of "what if" — and they are not the same.

"What if I try  and it doesn't work out?" You'll be fine. You will learn a lot if you try hard. You'll become a smarter and more resilient person than before.

"What if I had tried?" — that one has no answer. Ever. You will still ask yourself that question when you're too old to try.

> **[SLIDE: Bold text: "Not starting — that's the only real failure."]**

Trying and not reaching your goal is never a true loss. Not starting — that's the only real failure. Because it's the only outcome that gives you nothing. No result. No knowledge. No growth.

### PRINCIPLE 3 — THE FINAL BOSS

> **[9:00 – 11:30]**
> **[SLIDE: Photo of Fré on the podium — World Cup medal ceremony.]**

The third principle is about motivation

I've won World Cup gold twice. I can tell you exactly what that feels like.

It feels incredible... for about five minutes.

I'm serious. You're on the stage, the crowd is roaring, they are all watching you, someone hangs a medal around your neck, You're standing there humming the anthem because—let's be honest—you've forgotten half the lyrics. But then, the music stops.

You walk off that stage, you go backstage, and... that's it. The medal ceremony hasn't changed who you are. It hasn't solved your problems. The "vibe" is over.

> **[SLIDE: Plain text: "An end-goal has no intrinsic value."]**

The reason is simple: An end-goal has no intrinsic value.

Whether it's a gold medal, a job title, a revenue target, or a degree—it's just a marker. If that marker is your only motivation, you're chasing a hollow object. So I believe you should chase another underlying goal that stays with you for the rest of your life and something gamers call XP or Experience points. It's the experience, the skills you learned, the resilience you forged and the friendships you made that stay with you long after the trophy is on a shelf or the final boss is beaten.

> **[SLIDE: XP bar graphic, partially filled. Label: "XP — Experience Points."]**

For me the real value isn't the golden medal; it's the person I had to become to be capable of winning it.

Every brutal training session? XP. Every competition where I didn't win? XP. The network of climbers, coaches, and sponsors I have built? XP The self-confidence forged on every climbing move that I finally managed after trying hard at the time. That is XP.

> **[SLIDE: Same XP bar, now nearly full. Text alongside: "It stacks."]**

The beauty of XP is that it's cumulative. It stacks. It doesn't disappear when the ceremony ends. Even more, it enables opportunities. Sometimes even opportunities you couldn't foresee while you were building the required XP.

> **[SLIDE: LA 2028 Paralympic Games logo or striking LA skyline. Bold text overlay: "THE FINAL BOSS."]**

For example, Climbing was only confirmed to become Paralympics last year—and for me, Los Angeles 2028 is the Final Boss. It's the ultimate challenge in my sport. But here's the thing: [for most of the years I have been training, I never saw it as a realistic] you don't just walk up to a Final Boss and hope for the best. You need the skills. You need knowledge. You need a decade of leveling up.

If I hadn't been collecting that XP throughout a decade, I wouldn't be standing here ready for the challenge. I'd be under-leveled and overwhelmed.

> **[SLIDE: XP bar full. Text: "You can never truly lose."]**

So: be motivated by the XP you gain, not an imaginary end-goal. That's the mindset. If you focus on the XP, you can never truly lose and on top of that the XP will prepare you for even larger challenges that one day might come across your way.

### PRINCIPLE 4 — FUN disables Gravity

> **[11:30 – 13:30]**
> **[SLIDE: Two static gauges side by side — "DISCIPLINE" nearly empty (red) and "FUN" full (green).]**

What motivation mostly gives you is the energy you need to be disciplined — where discipline is the ability to push through the parts of the process that aren't naturally energising, for me that's eating healthy, staying away from candy and burgers.

If discipline would be a muscle, it's definitely important to train it. But here's the problem: discipline costs energy. You can endure your way through something hard for a while — but it's hard to maintain. Discipline alone will eventually take all your energy and run you out.

The solution to this is passion. Or, to use another word for it: having fun.

> **[SLIDE: French quote in large elegant type: "Talent c'est dans l'envie." — with translation below: "Talent is in the desire."]**

There's a French saying — "Talent c'est dans l'envie" — talent is in the desire. And I think that's true. Of course in sports you have physical talents, like being tall for basketball, but another type of talent is to find joy in the process. I believe that's the secret why some people get really good at certain things. They don't need to waste unnecessary energy on being disciplined, because they love to do what is required to become good. And the energy they save with that they can spend extra on becoming the best. Not having to be disciplined is like climbing with gravity disabled. It becomes way easier.

> **[SLIDE: Bold text: "FUN disables Gravity."]**

Fun is like an endless supply of gasoline for your engine.

But this also asks something of you — honesty. Real, uncomfortable honesty with yourself. Because from time to time, you have to pause and ask: is this still fun? Not the easy parts — the process. The daily grind. The parts nobody sees. And here's the thing: you often can't know the answer before you start. You only really find out once you're in it, once you've been doing it long enough for the novelty to wear off. That's when the truth shows up.

> **[SLIDE: Single question, centred: "Is this still fun?"]**

And if the answer is no — if the process drains you more than it drives you — then that's not a failure. That's information. It's okay to put down one dream and pick up another. Finding the thing that genuinely fuels you isn't giving up. It's the whole point.
